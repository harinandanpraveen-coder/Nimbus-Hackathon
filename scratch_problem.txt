 TEAM – Fork0ffCross-Organizational Flight Refund ResolutionThe ProblemWhen a traveler requests a flight refund, the case can span multiple independent organizations—such as a booking platform, airline, payment provider, and issuing bank. Each organization maintains its own records, identifiers, workflows, and refund statuses.The result is a fragmented view of what is actually happening.A booking platform may show a refund as Pending, while the airline shows it as Authorized, and the payment provider may have no matching refund transaction. These records may all refer to the same underlying transaction, but there is often no single operational view that connects them.From the customer's perspective, the question is simple:“Where is my refund?”For a support or operations team, answering it can require manually searching across multiple systems, reconciling different identifiers, interpreting inconsistent statuses, determining what has already happened, and figuring out which organization should act next.The deeper problem is therefore not simply that refunds take time. It is the lack of an explainable, end-to-end view of a refund case across organizational boundaries.Our goal is to create a reconciliation layer that does not replace existing systems of record, but connects their evidence into one unified case—showing what happened, where the case is stuck, who needs to act next, and why.Key Constraints:Limited access to external systems – Airlines, booking platforms, and payment providers may not expose their internal APIs. For the prototype, we should use mock/simulated systems and design MCP connectors that can later connect to real APIs.Identifier mismatches – Each organization may use different booking, order, and transaction IDs. The system must reconcile records using multiple attributes such as passenger, flight, date, amount, and timestamps rather than relying on a single ID.Uncertain matches – Records cannot always be confidently linked. The system must support an “Unresolved” state instead of forcing ownership when evidence is insufficient.Conflicting status definitions – Pending, Authorized, Initiated, Processing, and Completed can mean different things across organizations. A common internal refund lifecycle is needed to normalize them.LLM reliability – The LLM should interpret and explain structured evidence, not be the source of truth. Deterministic matching, state tracking, and business rules should establish the actual case state.Data privacy and security – Real cases may contain names, contact information, booking details, and payment information. The prototype should use synthetic data; a production system would require strong authentication, authorization, encryption, and audit controls.Timing and synchronization – Different systems may update at different times, so different statuses do not automatically mean something is wrong. Timestamps and expected processing windows must be considered.Human oversight – The AI should recommend the next owner/action rather than automatically issue refunds or make irreversible decisions. Low-confidence cases should be escalated for human review.And the system therefore transforms:Fragmented records → Unified case → Reconciled state → Identified blocker → Next responsible ownerStatistics:
